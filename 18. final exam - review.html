Chapter 1

Network Security Incidents

The first three weeks of this course taught you what to do when the attacker was quiet — phishing, malware, web-app injection. Chapter 1 is what happens when the attacker stops caring who notices. Loud network attacks, multiple sources, headlines.

Chapter 1 · Sub-Chapter 1.1

Categorise Network-Based Attacks: DoS, DDoS, MITM, Port Scanning, and Unauthorized Access

A network-based attack targets the paths between systems, not the systems themselves. Five families dominate the picture and they will appear on your Final in some combination on roughly every page.

DoS (Denial-of-Service). One attacker, one source IP (Internet Protocol) address, one target. The textbook example is the SYN (Synchronize) flood — the attacker opens TCP (Transmission Control Protocol) handshakes and never finishes them, slowly exhausting the server's connection table. Loud, traceable, defensible at one upstream hop.

DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service). Same goal, many sources. A botnet of compromised devices hammers the target in parallel. The October 2016 attack on Dyn used the Mirai botnet of compromised IoT cameras and DVRs (Digital Video Recorders) and knocked Twitter, Spotify, GitHub, Netflix and Reddit offline across the East Coast for several hours. That incident is the canonical example because the scale was unprecedented for the time and because nobody saw "your grandmother's baby monitor" as part of an attack chain.

MITM (Man-in-the-Middle). The attacker sits between two parties and either listens (sniffing) or rewrites (injection). At a coffee shop in downtown Montréal, an attacker on the open Wi-Fi can use ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) spoofing to convince your laptop they are the router; from then on every unencrypted byte is theirs. This is why TLS (Transport Layer Security) matters and why the little padlock in the browser is not decoration.

Port scanning. Reconnaissance, not destruction — but almost always the first move of something larger. The attacker probes a range of ports on a host to see what is open. TCP SYN scans, UDP (User Datagram Protocol) scans, ACK (Acknowledgment) scans, and Xmas scans behave differently and reveal different things. The dominant scanners in the wild are Nmap (Network Mapper) and Masscan. One scan from one external IP against your edge firewall is background noise; a focused scan from one internal source across hundreds of your internal hosts is a five-alarm fire.

Unauthorized access. Somebody got in who should not be in. Stolen credentials, an exposed RDP (Remote Desktop Protocol) port, a misconfigured VPN (Virtual Private Network), an unpatched VPN appliance, or plain social engineering. The hallmark question is the one nobody wants to answer at three in the morning: why is this account logging in from Ulaanbaatar at 03:14 EDT (Eastern Daylight Time)?

The critical insight. The five categories overlap, and a noisy DDoS is often a smokescreen for a quiet unauthorized-access pivot. Train yourself to ask "what else might be happening right now?" whenever a loud attack lands.

Chapter 1 · Sub-Chapter 1.2

Map Network Attack Vectors and the Exploitation Techniques Used at Each Layer

The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) model, standardised by ISO (International Organization for Standardization), describes how data moves through a network in seven layers. Real networks use the simpler TCP/IP four-layer model, but the OSI model stays as the conceptual map every defender uses, because attackers can strike at any layer and the defences are layer-specific.

Layer Name Examples of traffic Representative attacks
7 Application HTTP, HTTPS, DNS, SMTP, FTP SQL injection, XSS, malicious uploads
6 Presentation TLS, encoding, compression Protocol-downgrade attacks
5 Session Dialogue between apps Session hijacking, cookie theft
4 Transport TCP, UDP SYN floods, UDP reflection, port scans
3 Network IP, ICMP (Internet Control Message Protocol), routing IP spoofing, Smurf, ICMP redirect
2 Data Link Ethernet, Wi-Fi, ARP, MAC (Media Access Control) ARP poisoning, MAC flooding, VLAN hopping
1 Physical Cables, radio, hardware Wire-tapping, jamming, rogue APs (Access Points)

The crucial mental model: an attacker chooses the lowest layer where they can succeed, because lower-layer attacks bypass higher-layer defences. A WAF (Web Application Firewall) at Layer 7 will not save you from a rogue switch in the wiring closet at Layer 1. TLS at Layer 6 will not save you from ARP poisoning at Layer 2 — the poisoning happens before the TLS handshake. This is why defence-in-depth is not a slogan; it is the only way to cover the layers a single best control cannot reach.

The single industry-standard catalogue of attacker behaviours by tactic is the MITRE ATT&CK framework. Learn its names; you will see them on every threat-intel feed for the rest of your career.

Chapter 1 · Sub-Chapter 1.3

Perform Initial Detection and Triage Using Logs and Alerts

Triage is the moment the alarm fires and you must decide three things quickly: is it real, how bad is it, and what is the scope?

The raw materials of detection are logs and alerts, glued together by a SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) platform. Names you will encounter in the wild include Splunk, IBM QRadar, Microsoft Sentinel, Elastic Security and the open-source Wazuh.

The key log sources you will be expected to recognise:

  • Firewall logs — allows, drops, NAT (Network Address Translation) translations.
  • IDS/IPS logs — Snort, Suricata, Zeek (formerly Bro).
  • NetFlow / IPFIX (IP Flow Information Export) — who talked to whom, how much, when.
  • DNS query logs — DNS is the snitch. Malware almost always resolves a domain.
  • Authentication logs — Windows Event ID 4624 (logon), 4625 (failed logon), 4768 (Kerberos TGT — Ticket-Granting Ticket); Linux /var/log/auth.log.
  • Web server logs — access.log, error.log from Apache or Nginx.
  • EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) — CrowdStrike Falcon, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, SentinelOne.

The triage framework most analysts use is 4Ws + 1H:

  • What happened? (DoS, brute force, port scan, etc.)
  • When did it start, and is it still happening?
  • Where is the affected asset (and what is its business value)?
  • Who is the source — internal or external? Single IP or many?
  • How confident are we — is the signal corroborated by multiple sources?

Severity is then a function of confidence × scope × business impact. A high-confidence alert on your payment-processing server during business hours is a P1 (Priority 1). A medium-confidence alert on a developer laptop on Sunday is a P3. Decide fast, document the decision, and move.

Chapter 1 · Sub-Chapter 1.4

Coordinate with ISPs and External Parties During Large-Scale or Multi-Vector Attacks

There is a hard truth in incident response: when the attack is bigger than your bandwidth, you cannot win on your own. A 100 Gbps DDoS aimed at a 1 Gbps office uplink does not die at your firewall — it dies on your ISP's (Internet Service Provider's) edge, or it does not die at all. Coordination is therefore not optional, and every IH&R (Incident Handling & Response) plan must carry named, current contact information for upstream providers.

Who you call, and roughly in what order:

  • Your ISP's NOC (Network Operations Centre) and abuse desk. They can implement upstream filtering, advertise BGP (Border Gateway Protocol) blackholes, and re-route via RTBH (Remote Triggered Black Hole) filtering. Major Canadian ISPs include Bell, Rogers, TELUS and Cogeco. In the United States: AT&T Business, Verizon Business, and Lumen.

  • DDoS scrubbing services. Cloudflare Magic Transit, Akamai Prolexic, AWS Shield Advanced, Radware, Imperva. These services re-route your traffic through their network, scrub the bad packets, and deliver the clean traffic back to you.

  • National CSIRT (Computer Security Incident Response Team) / CERT (Computer Emergency Response Team). In Canada: the Canadian Centre for Cyber Security (CCCS, a part of CSE — Communications Security Establishment). In the United States: CISA (Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency). In the European Union: ENISA (European Union Agency for Cybersecurity). Globally: FIRST (Forum of Incident Response and Security Teams).

  • Law enforcement. In Canada: the RCMP National Cybercrime Coordination Centre (NC3). In the United States: the FBI IC3 (Internet Crime Complaint Center) or the local FBI field office.

  • Regulators and breach-notification authorities. In Canada: the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada (OPC) under PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act). In Québec: the CAI (Commission d'accès à l'information) under Law 25. In the United States, for health data: HHS OCR (Office for Civil Rights) under HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act). For certain critical-infrastructure sectors: mandatory reporting under CIRCIA (Cyber Incident Reporting for Critical Infrastructure Act).

  • Cyber-insurance carrier. The carrier has an IR (Incident Response) retainer team and a 24-hour hotline. Calling them early often unlocks free forensics support.

Order matters. Stop the bleeding (ISP / scrubbing) before you call the regulator. Communication during a multi-vector attack should be time-stamped, authenticated (use known-good channels, never the attacker's possibly-spoofed email), out-of-band where possible (pre-staged Signal or WhatsApp groups), and routed through a single designated spokesperson.

Chapter 1 · Sub-Chapter 1.5

Apply Eradication and Recovery Strategies to Restore Network Integrity Post-Incident

Eradication and Recovery are the third and fourth phases of the NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 IR lifecycle — Preparation → Detection & Analysis → Containment, Eradication, Recovery → Post-Incident Activity. The SANS Institute model splits them into separate steps; either way, the work is the same.

Eradication asks: what did the attacker leave behind, and how do we make sure none of it remains?

  • Remove malware binaries, dropper scripts, scheduled tasks, cron jobs.
  • Delete persistence — Windows Run keys, Linux systemd timers, WMI (Windows Management Instrumentation) event subscriptions, Office macro autoload hooks. Persistence is the MITRE ATT&CK TA0003 tactic; attackers plant multiple mechanisms specifically to survive a partial cleanup.
  • Revoke any account the attacker created or hijacked. Force broad password resets where appropriate.
  • Patch the vulnerability that enabled entry. Eradication without patching is a delay, not a fix.
  • Rotate every secret in scope — API (Application Programming Interface) keys, service-account passwords, SSH (Secure Shell) keys, certificates. Assume any secret accessible on any compromised host is compromised.

Recovery asks: how do we safely get back to normal operations?

  • Restore from known-good backups. The 3-2-1 backup rule is the bedrock: three copies, two different media, one off-site.
  • Bring services back gradually — least sensitive first, business-critical last. This is phased recovery.
  • Verify each system before reconnection: AV (Antivirus) scan, EDR clean, vulnerability scan, integrity check (Tripwire, AIDE, Get-FileHash on Windows).
  • Monitor closely for re-entry. A common attacker move is to come back through a different door 24–72 hours after you declare recovery.

Post-Incident Activity is the most-skipped and most-important phase. Schedule the lessons-learned meeting before you go home for the night. Without it, the same attack will work again in nine months.

Two specific legal points worth memorising:

  • Canada. Breaches of security safeguards involving personal information that pose a "real risk of significant harm" must be reported to the OPC under PIPEDA. In Québec, CAI notification under Law 25 has its own clock.
  • United States. The FBI IC3 accepts cybercrime reports; CISA receives voluntary reports through Cyber Incident Reporting; certain critical-infrastructure sectors have mandatory reporting under CIRCIA.
Chapter 2

Insider Threats

The shift in this chapter is uncomfortable on purpose: until now we have been training you to fight outsiders. From here on, the threat may already be inside the badge perimeter, sitting two desks away from you, with a valid payslip. The tools change. The legal posture changes. The HR (Human Resources) conversation — which never came up in the malware lecture — suddenly becomes the most important conversation in the room.

Chapter 2 · Sub-Chapter 2.1

Define the Three Insider-Threat Profiles: Malicious, Negligent, and Compromised

If asked on the corridor "what is an insider threat?", most students answer "a bad employee" — and that answer is wrong in a way that costs companies real money every quarter. There are three profiles, not one. The working definition borrowed from the Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) is the cleanest one out there: an insider threat is the risk that someone with authorised access uses that access — wittingly or unwittingly — to harm the organisation. Half of insider incidents are unwitting. That is where the three profiles come from.

Profile 1 — Malicious insider. Knows what they are doing and wants to do it. Motivated by a missed promotion, recruitment by a competitor, a side business, or revenge for an upcoming termination. Low volume of cases, very high cost per case — the IBM 2024 Cost of a Data Breach report put the average malicious-insider event at just under five million US dollars. See IBM X-Force threat intelligence for the recent year-on-year numbers.

Profile 2 — Negligent insider. Would never harm the company on purpose, but clicks the link, plugs in the random USB (Universal Serial Bus) stick from the parking lot, forgets to encrypt the laptop, sends the spreadsheet to the wrong distribution list. By count, this is the elephant — research from the Ponemon Institute consistently shows more than half of insider events come from this group.

Profile 3 — Compromised insider. Looks perfectly normal from inside the network because they are perfectly normal — they are also asleep. An attacker phished or infostealer-malware'd their way into the credentials and is now wearing the employee's identity like a mask. Your tools see a valid login. The user does not know it is happening.

Three profiles, same access, three different defences. Memorise the names and one defining sentence each.

Chapter 2 · Sub-Chapter 2.2

Recognise Behavioural and Technical Red Flags

Lesson 1 was about who; Lesson 2 is about what — what does each profile look like in the data? Carry one sentence out of this sub-chapter: "One red flag is gossip. Three correlated red flags in the same person, in the same window, across categories — that is an investigation."

Behavioural flags are about the human, observed by managers, colleagues and HR rather than by the SIEM:

  • Sudden disgruntlement after a missed promotion or performance review.
  • Sustained presence at unusual hours with no business reason.
  • Unexplained sudden affluence — a new car, a new watch, casual mention of paying down a mortgage.
  • Vocal resistance to security policies.
  • Refusal to take a mandatory vacation (vacations expose what people are hiding).
  • Open admiration of, or contact with, a competitor or hostile party.
  • Severe personal-life stressors paired with workplace withdrawal.
  • A resignation announcement in the last 60 days. The Carnegie Mellon CERT Insider Threat Center has shown that the highest-risk window in any tenure is the last 30 days before departure.

Technical flags are about the keyboard, observed by DLP (Data Loss Prevention), UEBA (User and Entity Behaviour Analytics) and IAM (Identity and Access Management) tools:

  • Mass downloads from internal file servers far above the user's normal volume.
  • Sudden use of personal USB drives on a corporate machine.
  • Outbound uploads to personal cloud storage — Dropbox, Google Drive, Mega, WeTransfer.
  • Logins outside normal hours or from new geolocations.
  • Bulk emails to a personal address with attachments.
  • Access to systems outside the person's job role.
  • Tampering with EDR or DLP agents.
  • Repeated failed logins followed by a sudden success at 02:00 on a Sunday.
  • First-time use of a dormant high-privilege account that has been unused for months.

Neither category alone is enough. A behavioural flag without a technical flag is rumour. A technical flag without context is noise. The bell rings when the two cross over inside one user inside one window.

Chapter 2 · Sub-Chapter 2.3

Implement Technical Controls: DLP, UEBA, and Access-Rights Monitoring

Three pillars of technology hold up any serious insider-threat program, and each one watches a different thing.

Pillar 1 — DLP (Data Loss Prevention). DLP watches data. It reads the actual content moving across your network, email, endpoint and cloud and matches it against policy — credit-card numbers, source-code repositories, classified labels, personal data subject to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Canadian personal data under PIPEDA, US health data under HIPAA, financial records under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX). When DLP sees a policy violation it can block, quarantine, or alert. The verbs matter: a blocking DLP rule that fires fifty false positives a day is worse than no DLP at all, because the team will quietly turn it off by Friday.

Pillar 2 — UEBA (User and Entity Behaviour Analytics). UEBA watches people. It learns what every user (and every server, service account, API key) normally does and scores deviations along many axes — time of day, day of week, asset class, geolocation, peer group, volume, sequence. Crucially, UEBA does not flag absolute behaviour ("Alice downloaded 4 GB") but relative behaviour ("Alice downloaded 4 GB on a Sunday when her peer group's median Sunday volume is 0.1 GB"). The math under the hood is the z-score, the standard statistical measure of how unusual one observation is.

Pillar 3 — Access-rights monitoring. Watches rights: who has access to what, since when, used or dormant, granted by whom, recertified or expired. The technologies here go by three letter-soups — IAM for the central directory and SSO (Single Sign-On), IGA (Identity Governance and Administration) for periodic recertification campaigns, PAM (Privileged Access Management) for the special "break-glass" accounts used by sysadmins and DBAs (Database Administrators). The principle of least privilege, codified in standards like NIST SP 800-53 from NIST, says grant the minimum access necessary and re-prove necessity on a schedule. Dormant high privilege is one of the top three predictors of an insider incident in every dataset that has ever been published.

The art of this sub-chapter is orchestration. Each pillar alone is a noisy alarm; together they become a defensible case file. Your SIEM glues them; the SOC interprets them; the Insider Threat Program (Sub-Chapter 2.5) owns them.

Chapter 2 · Sub-Chapter 2.4

Navigate the Legal, Ethical, and HR Considerations

This is the sub-chapter where the technology stops mattering and the people part starts mattering completely. Carry this sentence out of the room:

The moment a UEBA alert points at a named human being, every subsequent action you take must pass three gates — Legal, Ethical, and HR — and you must be able to prove, in writing, that all three gates were cleared.

Done well, an insider investigation ends with the right outcome and everyone moves on. Done badly, it ends in any of these places — all real headlines from the last decade: a wrongful-dismissal lawsuit, a privacy regulator fining you under GDPR or PIPEDA, an employee's union taking you to arbitration, a journalist getting the story, or worst, an employee who was innocent harming themselves because the company humiliated them publicly before checking the facts.

The Legal Gate.

  • Is there a documented lawful basis to monitor this user? Did they accept that monitoring in their employment contract, or via a banner the law in their jurisdiction recognises as consent?
  • Is the proposed monitoring action permitted by the applicable data-protection law — GDPR in the EU, PIPEDA in Canada, the patchwork of US state laws led by California's CCPA/CPRA?
  • Will evidence be collected under a documented chain of custody — who, when, hashes, sealed storage?
  • In countries that require it (France, Germany, the Netherlands and others), has the works council been notified?
  • Has counsel signed off — in writing, not in a corridor conversation?

The Ethical Gate.

  • Is the proposed action proportionate to the strength of suspicion, or are we deploying surveillance over one low-confidence alert?
  • Are we using the least-intrusive method that can still answer the question?
  • Have we actively checked for bias toward one demographic group? If your alert pipeline only ever flags the same kind of person, the pipeline has the problem, not the people.
  • Is the action reversible until guilt is established — no public-shaming, no preemptive comms?
  • Is the dignity of the employee preserved at every step?

The HR Gate.

  • Has the employee's normal performance record been reviewed — outlier or pattern?
  • Has mental-health and EAP (Employee Assistance Programme) support been considered as a first option where appropriate?
  • Will the disciplinary procedure published in the employee handbook be followed step by step?
  • Will the manager be briefed only on a need-to-know basis and reminded not to confront the employee — premature confrontation tips the suspect off and destroys evidence?
  • Is there a communications plan for the team when action becomes visible — sudden access removal, IT visit, an empty chair on Monday morning?

The structure that runs all three gates in parallel is the cross-functional panel — Security, Legal, HR, Communications, all in the same room. Both ISO/IEC 27035 (the international incident-management standard) and the CISA Insider Threat Mitigation Guide name the cross-functional panel as the only acceptable forum for investigative decisions. No single function gets to fire alone.

Chapter 2 · Sub-Chapter 2.5

Components of a Formal Insider Threat Program and a Security-Aware Culture

An ITP (Insider Threat Program) is not a tool. It is a sustained organisational program. Tools rust; programs renew themselves. Both the CISA Insider Threat Mitigation Guide and the US National Insider Threat Task Force (NITTF) describe an ITP as having the same five operational pillars on top of an executive sponsor and a culture foundation.

Layer 1 — Executive sponsor and governance. A single named executive — sometimes the CISO (Chief Information Security Officer), sometimes the CRO (Chief Risk Officer), sometimes the General Counsel — owns the program at board level. They sign the charter, defend the budget, and present the annual report to the board. They define the risk appetite (for example, "we will tolerate up to X negligent events per quarter; zero malicious data exfiltrations involving customer PII (Personally Identifiable Information)"). Without that sponsor the program dies the second the budget gets squeezed.

Layer 2 — Five operational pillars. All verbs.

  • Define. Write down what counts as an insider, which assets are crown jewels, what the risk tolerance is, who plays what role on the panel, and which policies every employee must sign (the "Acceptable Use Policy" click-through on Day 1 is the Define pillar's output).
  • Detect. Run the DLP / UEBA / IAM tooling from Sub-Chapter 2.3, plus a confidential tip line, periodic audits, and a programme of manager training so first-line managers can spot behavioural flags.
  • Assess. Run the cross-functional panel from Sub-Chapter 2.4. The Assess pillar is the human bottleneck on every alert and is explicit, per CISA, that you evaluate behaviours, not profiles — never build a watchlist of demographics, build a watchlist of behaviours.
  • Manage. Decide what to do with each case. Intervene early where possible, use reversible actions first, offer EAP referral where appropriate, apply discipline by the book if needed, off-board cleanly if it ends in termination, refer to law enforcement in the rare cases that warrant it.
  • Learn. Every closed case becomes a PIR (Post-Incident Review). The PIR updates detection rules, training content, policies, and the program's own charter. A program without a Learn pillar gets exactly the same incident again next quarter.

Layer 3 — Culture foundation. The unsung half. Seven traits a healthy ITP quietly builds:

  • Trust plus verify — assume good faith, build controls anyway.
  • Psychological safety — people can raise concerns without retaliation.
  • A just-culture model — borrowed from aviation safety. Mistakes are coached, patterns are managed, only deliberate harm is punished.
  • Manager involvement — the first-line manager is the most important detector in the entire program.
  • Awareness training — annual, role-specific, never a 30-minute video.
  • Onboarding and off-boarding hygiene — Day 1 and the last day are the two highest-risk moments in any employee tenure.
  • Wellbeing and EAP — many insider incidents trace back to gambling debt, addiction, untreated mental-health conditions, or sudden personal crises. Reference programmes: SANS Security Awareness and the UK's NPSA (formerly CPNI — Centre for the Protection of National Infrastructure).

Single sentence to leave with: Insider threats are not a tool problem. They are a people problem solved by tools, by process, by law and by culture — in that order, all at once, with a panel of grown-ups in the room.

Chapter 3

Endpoint Security: Mobile, IoT, and OT

So far in this course you have walked the room — malware, phishing, the web tier, the network, the people inside the badge perimeter. Chapter 3 walks to the edge. The phones in your pocket. The cameras over the door. The little grey box that opens the valve when the tank gets too hot. All of them are endpoints, none of them behave like a laptop, and that is the whole point.

The cold open you will remember: in November 2017 a North American casino lost roughly 10 GB (gigabytes) of high-roller player records. The breach started not at a server, not at a laptop, but at an internet-connected fish-tank thermometer sitting in the lobby — an IoT (Internet of Things) sensor that a vendor had placed on the corporate Wi-Fi so it could text maintenance when the water got too cold. The attacker found the thermometer, used it as a beachhead, and pivoted laterally through a flat network into the high-roller database. The IT (Information Technology) team did not know the thermometer existed; the security team's tools did not run on it; there was no agent to install, no patch to deploy, no logs to read.

Chapter 3 · Sub-Chapter 3.1

Unique Security Challenges of Mobile, IoT, and OT/ICS

Three families. On a network diagram they look superficially alike — little boxes with IP addresses — but the moment you sit down to defend one, the families have almost nothing in common.

Mobile. The family with a human owner. Phones, tablets, the company laptop your CEO never closes. Crown jewels: corporate email, MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication) push approvals, the camera, the microphone, the GPS (Global Positioning System) chip. Threat models: smishing (SMS — Short Message Service — phishing), malicious sideloaded apps, lost or stolen hardware, and at the high end the zero-click chains sold by surveillance vendors. Patch cadence is the best of the three families — Apple and Google push monthly updates, and a well-run MDM (Mobile Device Management) programme forces them in days.

IoT. The family with no clear owner. Lobby cameras, smart bulbs, badge readers, conference-room sound bars, the HVAC (Heating, Ventilation, and Air Conditioning) sensor that someone in facilities asked the vendor to install on the corporate VLAN (Virtual Local Area Network). The crown jewels look small — a temperature reading, a few seconds of video — but the device itself is often the prize. IoT is where botnets recruit. Mirai conscripted hundreds of thousands of IoT cameras in 2016 by trying the list of 61 default username/password pairs the manufacturers had hard-coded into their devices. Patch cadence is brutal: most IoT devices receive one firmware update in their lifetime, if any.

OT (Operational Technology) and ICS (Industrial Control Systems). The family that controls physical reality. PLCs (Programmable Logic Controllers) running ladder logic on a factory floor. SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) consoles. HMIs (Human-Machine Interfaces) next to a turbine. The crown jewels are not data — they are physics: steel temperatures, centrifuge rotation speeds, the chemical balance of water leaving a municipal treatment plant. When OT breaks, people downstream stop drinking water. The reference incident is the 2017 Triton/TRISIS attack, where attackers targeted a chemical plant's Safety Instrumented System — they wanted the safety layer to fail silent so the next process upset would not be caught. Patch cadence: yearly during a scheduled turnaround if you are lucky; otherwise, never.

The thing that unites the three families is also the thing that makes them dangerous: they live on the same network as your laptop, but you cannot defend them the same way. No EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) agent will ever run on a PLC. No SOC (Security Operations Center) analyst will remote into a smart bulb. The defences come from the network around the device, the lifecycle around the device, and the standards that govern who is allowed to touch the device.

Chapter 3 · Sub-Chapter 3.2

Common Vulnerabilities and the Expanded Attack Surface

A 1995 corporate network ran on the order of one IP-connected device per employee. A 2025 corporate network runs somewhere between five and twenty, depending on how you count. Every printer is a device, every conference-room control panel, every meeting-room camera, every door reader, every UPS (Uninterruptible Power Supply), every smart bulb. The badge you tap to get into the building has a microcontroller that talks back to a server.

The standard catalogue of weaknesses on endpoint-rich devices reads like a 1998 pen-test report:

  • Default credentials. admin/admin, root/root. Mirai used the list of 61 hard-coded pairs cited above.
  • Unauthenticated management interfaces. The device serves a web UI (User Interface) on port 80 that anyone on the LAN (Local Area Network) can browse without logging in.
  • Unsigned firmware. Push any blob with the right command and the device reflashes itself — no verification, no rollback.
  • Cleartext protocols. Modbus, DNP3 (Distributed Network Protocol 3) and BACnet are routinely deployed without authentication or encryption. A passive listener on the wire reads setpoints in real time.
  • Hardcoded backdoor accounts. "Service" or "support" accounts the vendor never told the customer about, occasionally surfaced in CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures) advisories. Browse the history of CISA Cybersecurity Advisories for an entertainingly grim list.
  • No logging or telemetry the customer can read. Whatever happens on the device stays on the device.

Combine that catalogue with a flat network — guest Wi-Fi reachable from sensor VLAN, sensor VLAN reachable from printer VLAN, printer VLAN reachable from finance — and you have the actual configuration in roughly half of mid-sized companies. The aquarium thermometer story is the canonical illustration: the device was almost beside the point. The attacker needed only one weakly-defended speck on a network that did not believe in segmentation, and the high-roller database was four hops away.

The takeaway for an incident handler: the question is rarely whether you have an exposed weak device — you do. The question is whether your network design contains the blast radius.

Chapter 3 · Sub-Chapter 3.3

Adapt Incident Response Procedures for Resource-Constrained and Legacy Endpoints

The IR (Incident Response) playbook you have been studying — Preparation → Identification → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned, per NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 — was written assuming a normal computer. It assumes you can deploy an agent, pull a memory image, re-image the host, and take it offline for an hour. None of those assumptions hold on a PLC, an HVAC sensor, or a payment terminal whose franchise owner cannot touch it without voiding the warranty. The playbook does not get thrown out; it gets adapted.

Preparation stops being about pushing an EDR agent and starts being about positioning the network to see the device. Span ports on the switch closest to the OT cell. Syslog feeds from the vendor's cloud console. NetFlow on the upstream firewall. You collect the telemetry that flows past the device, not from it.

Identification correlates that ambient telemetry with two extra channels: the vendor's cloud console (if one exists) and the engineering workstation logs. The PLC will not tell you it was attacked. The engineering workstation that the attacker compromised to talk to the PLC absolutely will.

Containment is the phase where the gap is widest. The classic move is to isolate the host. On an OT system, you cannot — pulling a PLC offline can trip a safety system and stop production. The adapted move is to quarantine the path, not the device: change a switch ACL (Access Control List), micro-segment the conduit, drop the C2 (Command and Control) channel at the firewall. The device stays running; the attacker's reach is cut.

Eradication is where the difference is sharpest. There is nothing to "remove" from a PLC. Its firmware is the operating system, the application, and the persistence all baked into one blob. Eradication on constrained gear means reflash to a known-good vendor image, factory reset, rotate any shared cryptographic material. It is a coordinated change with the vendor on the call; it is not a taskkill /F.

Recovery is governed by the operations team, not by you. The plant has a turnaround schedule. The hospital has a surgery list. The data centre has an availability target. Your job is to be ready for the window the business gives you.

Lessons Learned has one extra step that almost nobody writes in their playbook: fix the asset inventory first. Nine times out of ten the device that got compromised was not on the list. If you do not update the list, the next incident hits the next device that is not on the list. You cannot defend what you never inventoried.

The volatile-data collection order, when you do get to triage a constrained box, follows RFC 3227 — Guidelines for Evidence Collection and Archiving. Most volatile first (registers, cache, RAM — Random-Access Memory), least volatile last (archival media). The reverse of that order destroys evidence.

Chapter 3 · Sub-Chapter 3.4

Evaluate MDM Platforms and EDR Tools

Two acronyms get used interchangeably by people who should know better. They are not the same thing, they are not competitors, and the overlap is smaller than the marketing slides claim. Most mature environments need both, configured deliberately so they cover different gaps.

MDM (Mobile Device Management). Owns the device's identity and posture. Enrolment. Inventory. App-store policy. Passcode requirements. Encryption-at-rest enforcement. Remote wipe. Geofencing. Jailbreak and root detection. VPN and Wi-Fi profile push. Major platforms in North America: Microsoft Intune, Jamf (Apple-heavy shops), and VMware Workspace ONE. The conceptual generalisation UEM (Unified Endpoint Management) — managing not just phones but laptops too — has largely eaten the "MDM" label, but the verb is the same: manage.

EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response). Owns the device's behaviour. Process telemetry. Behavioural detection. Memory scanning. File-and-registry IOCs (Indicators of Compromise). Live-response shell. Network containment. Threat-hunting queries. Timeline reconstruction after an incident. Major platforms: CrowdStrike Falcon, Microsoft Defender for Endpoint, SentinelOne, Sophos Intercept X, Trend Micro Apex One. The verb here is detect and respond, which is literally the acronym.

The overlap is the seven-item list every procurement exercise should start from — both tools maintain an asset inventory, produce compliance reports, supply a posture signal to conditional-access decisions, forward logs to a SIEM, support role-based admin, ship dashboards, and integrate with a help-desk system. The procurement mistake is to buy the same coverage twice in the overlap zone and then leave the EDR-only or MDM-only zone unfilled because the budget was spent.

For your career, internalise the difference in one sentence: "MDM posture says the phone is encrypted, passcode-protected, and not jailbroken; EDR behaviour says the phone is calling out to a known C2 IP at 03:00." Two completely different sentences about the same device. A good security programme needs both being true at the same time.

Chapter 3 · Sub-Chapter 3.5

Key Regulatory Standards for IoT and OT Security: IEC 62443 and NIST SP 800-82

You will not memorise these documents. Nobody does. But you have to know what they are, who publishes them, what they are for, and roughly which section to flip to when a customer asks "are we compliant?"

IEC 62443 is published by the International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) in partnership with the International Society of Automation (ISA). It is the international standard for Industrial Automation and Control Systems Security and is the document OT vendors themselves use when they design and certify their products. The family has four parts:

  • 62443-1-x — terminology and concepts. "What is an IACS (Industrial Automation and Control System)?"
  • 62443-2-x — what the owner-operator of an industrial site has to do: policies, programs, patch management.
  • 62443-3-x — what the system integrator designs into a deployment: zones, conduits, security levels.
  • 62443-4-x — what the product vendor has to bake into a PLC or HMI to certify it.

The single most useful sub-standard for an incident handler is IEC 62443-3-3, which lists System Requirements (SRs) grouped into seven Foundational Requirements labelled FR1 through FR7 — identification and authentication control, use control, system integrity, data confidentiality, restricted data flow, timely response to events, and resource availability. They map almost one-for-one to the NIST SP 800-53 families.

NIST SP 800-82 is published by NIST as the Guide to Operational Technology Security. Revision 3 (2023) deliberately broadened its scope from "ICS" to "OT" to cover IoT, building automation, and medical-device-style endpoints under one umbrella. It is not a competitor to IEC 62443 — it is a tailoring guide that explains how to apply the NIST SP 800-53 controls family in an OT setting. For United States federal work, an auditor hands you 800-82. For certified-product work for industrial customers internationally, the customer hands you IEC 62443. In practice you cite both.

A third document worth recognising by name: ETSI EN 303 645, the European baseline for consumer IoT, published by the European Telecommunications Standards Institute (ETSI). It is the source of the "no default passwords" rule the IoT marketing copy now routinely cites. Background reading worth bookmarking includes NIST SP 800-124r2 (Mobile Device Security) for mobile and RFC 5737, published under IANA (Internet Assigned Numbers Authority) authority, which reserves the 192.0.2.0/24 range used as a safe target for documentation-only lab scans.

The lesson under the standards is the lesson under every other sub-chapter of this chapter: endpoints are not what they used to be, and the IR playbook bends to fit them, not the other way around.

Case Study 1

The Habs Ticket Drop

Covers: Chapter 1 (Network Security Incidents), Sub-Chapter 1.1 (Categorise Network-Based Attacks)

Scenario. It is 09:55 on a Tuesday in February in Montréal. MaplePuckTickets.ca, a small third-party reseller, is releasing playoff tickets for the Montréal Canadiens at 10:00. The site lives in a single Toronto data centre and normally serves 200 concurrent users. At 09:58 the SOC analyst on duty, Jasmine, gets her first PagerDuty alert: outbound network throughput is sitting at 98 %. By 10:00 the web server is unresponsive and her dashboard is on fire — millions of HTTP (HyperText Transfer Protocol) GET requests per second from tens of thousands of distinct IP addresses. Jasmine starts the DDoS playbook: call the upstream ISP, enable Cloudflare's "Under Attack" mode, escalate to the on-call manager. Two desks over, Mateo notices something quieter: a single corporate VPN account belonging to the database administrator logged in successfully at 10:02 from a residential IP in Bucharest, Romania. The admin is sitting next to Mateo in Montréal, eating a bagel.

Question. Categorise the loud event and the quiet event. Which category was the actual incident? What single mental habit, taught in Sub-Chapter 1.1, would have made Jasmine slower to be deceived?

Most-possible answer. The loud event is a DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) — many sources, single target, throughput-saturating volumetric pattern. The quiet event is unauthorized access via credential stuffing or credential theft against the VPN, evidenced by a successful login from a geolocation the legitimate user is not in. The DDoS was the smokescreen; the unauthorised access was the real incident, and the actual cost was the exfiltration of 180,000 customer payment records while attention was elsewhere. The single habit that would have saved her is the one we said belongs in your reflex: when a noisy attack lands, ask "what else might be happening right now?" The five attack categories overlap; a senior analyst treats every loud event as a candidate distraction until cross-traffic is verified.

Case Study 2

The Coffee Shop Wi-Fi Audit

Covers: Chapter 1 (Network Security Incidents), Sub-Chapter 1.2 (Map Network Attack Vectors and Exploitation Techniques per Layer)

Scenario. You have a one-day audit at Café Maple Leaf, a small independent coffee shop in Toronto's Kensington Market. They have a guest Wi-Fi network (no password), a staff Wi-Fi network (WPA2 — Wi-Fi Protected Access 2 — password taped to the modem), a point-of-sale (POS) terminal connected by Ethernet to a small switch, a back-office laptop on the staff Wi-Fi running QuickBooks, and a modem from Bell Canada providing internet. The router admin page is reachable from guest Wi-Fi with the default admin/admin credentials.

Question. Walk the network top-down through the seven OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) layers. Name one realistic attack a stranger could attempt at each layer. Which single network-design change would shrink the blast radius across multiple layers at once?

Most-possible answer.

  • Layer 1 (Physical) — modem and switch in an unlocked cupboard under the counter: plug in a rogue device, photograph the WPA2 password the staff taped to the modem.
  • Layer 2 (Data Link) — flat switch, no port security, no VLAN separation: ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) poisoning to MITM (Man-in-the-Middle) the POS terminal.
  • Layer 3 (Network) — single flat 192.168.0.0/24 subnet: ICMP (Internet Control Message Protocol) redirect or IP (Internet Protocol) spoofing against the POS.
  • Layer 4 (Transport) — no firewall between guest and staff networks: port-scan the back-office laptop from the guest Wi-Fi.
  • Layer 5 (Session) — QuickBooks session left open, cookie not bound to IP: session hijacking when the back-office laptop steps away.
  • Layer 6 (Presentation) — POS still uses TLS (Transport Layer Security) 1.0 to its payment gateway: force a downgrade attack and decrypt card data.
  • Layer 7 (Application) — router admin page reachable from guest Wi-Fi with default admin/admin: log in and reconfigure DNS (Domain Name System) to redirect everyone to a phishing site.

The single biggest blast-radius reduction is network segmentation — separate VLANs for guest Wi-Fi, staff devices, and the POS, with a firewall between them and explicit allow-rules. That one change alone breaks Layers 2 through 4 attacks and significantly limits the reach of a Layer 7 compromise.

Case Study 3

The Friday-Night Pizza Ticket

Covers: Chapter 1 (Network Security Incidents), Sub-Chapter 1.3 (Detection and Triage Using Logs and Alerts)

Scenario. Friday, 19:42. You are the on-shift Tier-1 SOC (Security Operations Center) analyst at an MSSP (Managed Security Service Provider) in Ottawa whose client is PacificFreight Co., a Vancouver-based logistics company. The SIEM (Security Information and Event Management) dashboard opens three tickets within a 90-second window:

  • #4471 — Medium. "Possible port scan from 185.220.x.x against the public web server (port 80, 443, 22, 3389, 8080 in a sweep)."
  • #4472 — High. "Authentication brute-force: 412 failed logins on the SSH (Secure Shell) service from 185.220.x.x within 60 seconds."
  • #4473 — Medium. "DNS anomaly: 47 NXDOMAIN responses from an internal workstation (10.40.12.55) in 30 seconds."

Question. Apply the 4Ws + 1H to each ticket. Which tickets should be merged, which should be split, and what is your final severity assignment? Where does the source IP detail (a Tor exit node) change your action?

Most-possible answer.

Tickets #4471 and #4472 share the same external source IP, the same target (the public web server, a P1 — Priority 1 — asset), the same time window, and a sequence that makes operational sense (scan first, then exploit). They are the same actor and the same incident, so merge them, escalate to Critical, and apply the firewall block on the source IP from the runbook. Time to escalate to Tier 2: under five minutes. The fact that the source is a Tor exit node raises confidence that this is targeted reconnaissance rather than scattered noise, and it also means an IP block will only stop this exit node — keep the alert open and watch for the same pattern from other exits.

Ticket #4473 is independent — different source (internal), different asset class (a P3 HR — Human Resources — workstation), different signature (DNS-based, potentially a C2 — Command and Control — beacon, possibly just a typo storm). Open a separate investigation. Pull the workstation's DNS query logs, check the host against EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response), and dispatch to Tier 2 with an evidence package. Severity stays Medium until the DNS responses are confirmed against threat-intel feeds.

Case Study 4

Black Friday at MapleMart

Covers: Chapter 1 (Network Security Incidents), Sub-Chapter 1.4 (Coordinate with ISPs and External Parties)

Scenario. Friday, November 28 — Black Friday. MapleMart.ca, a mid-sized Canadian e-commerce site headquartered in Mississauga, Ontario, normally pushes 800 Mbps peak. At 06:03 EST (Eastern Standard Time) throughput jumps to 8 Gbps and climbing. Origin web servers are still up, but every customer page-load takes 40+ seconds. By 06:15 throughput is 14 Gbps. Their commercial uplink is 10 Gbps. The incident commander, Priya, has 30 minutes before social media catches fire.

Question. Build the external-call escalation chain in the correct order, with the right talking points. What single mistake in the order would expose MapleMart to a regulator fine before the attack is even contained?

Most-possible answer. Stop the bleeding first; call the regulator last. The chain:

  1. 06:15 — Call Bell Business NOC (Network Operations Centre). Pre-staged number, pre-staged account ID. "We are under active DDoS. Confirm you see the traffic spike. Request upstream rate-limiting on ports 80 / 443 from non-Canadian source AS — Autonomous System — numbers." Bell logs the case and advertises a partial RTBH (Remote Triggered Black Hole).
  2. 06:18 — Call Cloudflare emergency line. Toggle "Under Attack" mode; enable Bot Fight Mode; switch DNS to Cloudflare proxied. Mitigations propagate in roughly three minutes.
  3. 06:25 — Open ticket with CCCS — Canadian Centre for Cyber Security. Voluntary, free for Canadian organisations, triggers intel sharing.
  4. 06:30 — Page the cyber-insurance hotline. The carrier's IR retainer firm joins the bridge.
  5. 06:45 — Notify OPC (Office of the Privacy Commissioner) only if there is evidence of personal-data exfiltration. So far none — log the decision in the timeline and move on.
  6. 07:00 — Update the public status page. Single spokesperson, no specifics on attack vectors.
  7. 08:30 — Mitigations holding. Begin post-incident timeline reconstruction.

The mistake that exposes MapleMart to a fine is notifying OPC before evidence of personal-data exfiltration exists. Under PIPEDA (Personal Information Protection and Electronic Documents Act) the trigger is "real risk of significant harm" to personal information. A premature notification — driven by panic rather than evidence — locks in a public record of breach where none yet exists, and can complicate subsequent disclosure obligations. Stop the bleeding, then notify based on what the evidence shows.

Case Study 5

The Morning After at PacificFreight

Covers: Chapter 1 (Network Security Incidents), Sub-Chapter 1.5 (Eradication and Recovery)

Scenario. Continuing from Case Study 3: the Tor-exit attacker succeeded on the third try with the SSH brute-force, escalated to root via an unpatched sudo CVE (Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures), installed a reverse-shell systemd service, and made off with a tarball of customer routing manifests. The IR team has stopped the bleeding by Saturday morning. They must fully eradicate and recover by Monday 06:00. Outline the plan.

Question. Build the eradication checklist, the phased recovery sequence, and the post-incident actions including regulator notification. Which step is most likely to be skipped, and what does NIST (National Institute of Standards and Technology) say about that step?

Most-possible answer.

Eradication checklist (Saturday afternoon).

  • Identify every host the attacker touched — last, wtmp, auth.log, EDR (Endpoint Detection and Response) process telemetry, NetFlow for the affected subnet.
  • Re-image the compromised web server from a known-good golden image, not from a backup of the running disk (the backup is suspect).
  • Search for sister persistence on every host that shares credentials or trust with the compromised server — new systemd units, cron entries, authorized_keys additions.
  • Rotate every SSH (Secure Shell) key on the perimeter. Rotate the API (Application Programming Interface) key the web server used to reach the database. Rotate the database admin password.
  • Patch the sudo CVE on every Linux host in scope, not just the compromised one.
  • Disable password-based SSH on internet-facing hosts; switch to key-only with fail2ban and CrowdSec.
  • Document each step with command-and-output evidence.

Recovery (Sunday).

  • Bring the rebuilt web server up disconnected from the internet first. Run a full Lynis audit and a ClamAV scan. Verify the SHA-256 (Secure Hash Algorithm 256-bit) hash of every binary in /usr/bin against a known-good baseline.
  • Reconnect to a sandboxed network. Run live traffic for 12 hours; if EDR sees no anomalies, proceed.
  • Re-introduce to production behind Cloudflare with strict WAF (Web Application Firewall) rules and rate limiting.
  • Switch DNS back. Declare recovery at Monday 03:00.

Post-incident (week following).

  • Lessons-learned meeting Tuesday 10:00, mandatory attendance.
  • File the OPC notification under PIPEDA — this incident is reportable because customer PII (Personally Identifiable Information) left the building.
  • File a voluntary report with CCCS to contribute IOCs (Indicators of Compromise) to national threat intel.
  • Update the runbook with the gaps the incident exposed.

The step most likely to be skipped is the lessons-learned meeting. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 calls it Post-Incident Activity and explicitly identifies it as the most-skipped and most-important phase — without it, the same attack works again in nine months.

Case Study 6

The Three Profiles at *RedMaple Retail*

Covers: Chapter 2 (Insider Threats), Sub-Chapter 2.1 (Three Insider-Threat Profiles)

Scenario. You receive three insider-related stories from one fictional mid-sized retailer in a single quarter. Label each.

  • Story A — Tom in Sales. Six years at the company, passed over for promotion in March. In April he polished his LinkedIn profile. The first week of May, at 19:42 on a Friday, he logged into the CRM (Customer Relationship Management) system and exported the entire EMEA (Europe, Middle East, and Africa) customer list to a CSV (Comma-Separated Values) file. Monday morning his resignation was on the manager's desk. The CSV is on a USB drive in his pocket.
  • Story B — Maria in Engineering. Twenty-six, brilliant, slightly chaotic. The team is shipping a release. At 02:14 she finishes a backup of the staging database to her laptop so she can keep working from home. The backup is not encrypted because the tool kept asking for a passphrase she could not remember and the build was going to fail without sleep. Next morning she gets pickpocketed on the train. Laptop and staging data leave together.
  • Story C — Igor in Marketing. Leads EMEA campaigns. Three weeks ago he received an email that looked like it came from the CFO (Chief Financial Officer) asking him to review the next quarter's marketing budget. He clicked, entered his credentials on a page that looked like the Microsoft 365 login. The URL (Uniform Resource Locator) said microsft365-secure with a missing letter; he did not notice. That night somebody in Bucharest signed in as Igor and quietly downloaded press releases two days before they went public, short-selling the stock through a relative's account. The UEBA (User and Entity Behaviour Analytics) tool finally flagged "Igor" logging in from two countries within an hour.

Question. Label each story with the correct profile and name the primary defence for each.

Most-possible answer.

  • Story A → Malicious insider. Clear motive (missed promotion + new job lined up), clear intent (out-of-hours bulk export of data outside any assigned project). Primary defences: DLP (Data Loss Prevention) blocking on bulk customer-list exports, leaver-monitoring in the last 30 days of tenure, mandatory-vacation policy that exposes anomalies, and an off-boarding checklist that revokes access on Day Zero.
  • Story B → Negligent insider. No malice, just a Tuesday afternoon problem made worse by a tool that fights the user. Primary defences: full-disk encryption enforced by MDM (Mobile Device Management) — not by the user remembering a passphrase, regular awareness training, and a usability fix to the encryption workflow so it does not become the rate-limiting step at 02:14.
  • Story C → Compromised insider. Igor did nothing wrong inside the network; an attacker did everything for him. Primary defences: phishing-resistant MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication, e.g., FIDO2 — Fast IDentity Online 2 — security keys), UEBA flagging impossible-travel logins, EDR detection of credential-stealer malware, and a "report phish" button that gives users a one-click way to alert SOC.
Case Study 7

Diego the Backend Engineer

Covers: Chapter 2 (Insider Threats), Sub-Chapters 2.2 (Behavioural and Technical Red Flags) and 2.4 (Legal, Ethical, and HR Considerations)

Scenario. Diego is a senior backend engineer at a fintech in Madrid. Two weeks ago he had a heated conversation with his manager about being passed over for the team-lead role. Nobody outside the team knows. On the following Sunday at 03:11 an authentication event shows Diego's account logging in from his home IP; the SIEM marks it informational. Three days later at 09:45 Diego's UEBA score climbs by 1.6 standard deviations because he pulled 4.2 GB (gigabytes) of code from the GitLab Enterprise server; he normally pulls 800 MB (megabytes) per week. DLP labels the files as internal-source-code/payment-rails. The next day at 11:20 the firewall logs an HTTPS (HyperText Transfer Protocol Secure) upload of 4.1 GB of compressed data to a personal Mega.nz account that Diego has never used from a corporate device. Four flags, two categories, one user, ten-day window.

Question. What is the minimum number of red flags from how many categories that should trigger the cross-functional investigation panel? Walk the panel's three gates (Legal, Ethical, HR) for Diego. What is the one technical action the panel should authorise before speaking to him?

Most-possible answer. The rule from the lecture is "three correlated red flags in the same person, in the same window, across categories." Categories means at least one behavioural plus at least one technical, not just three flags of the same type. Diego has the missed-promotion behavioural flag plus three technical flags (after-hours login, bulk download, anomalous upload) — that crosses the bar.

The panel — Security, Legal, HR, Communications — convenes without the manager.

  • Legal gate. Has Diego signed a monitoring clause? Yes, employment contract revised June 2025. Is Spain the relevant jurisdiction? Yes — both Diego's contract and the company's Agencia Española de Protección de Datos (AEPD) registration apply. Will evidence be preserved with hashes and a sealed chain of custody? Yes, logs were snapshotted at 09:05 with SHA-256 hashes. Counsel signs off in writing.
  • Ethical gate. Proportionate? Yes, the technical signal is strong and corroborated by behavioural context. Least-intrusive method? Recall the laptop under the asset-recovery clause; do not seize his phone. Biased? The same query was run for the rest of the team — three other engineers with missed promotions, none with the technical pattern — confidence is high the alert is on the right person. Reversible until forensics confirms? Yes, no internal announcement.
  • HR gate. Performance record? Strong — top performer three years running. Recent personal stressors? A documented recent divorce. EAP (Employee Assistance Programme) appropriate? Offer regardless of outcome. Disciplinary procedure followed step by step? Yes. Manager confronts Diego? Absolutely not, until after the conversation.

The single technical action authorised before the conversation: silently down-scope Diego's access to "investigation mode" (read-only, no exfiltration paths) and image the laptop during a pretextual fleet refresh. That action is reversible and preserves the dignity of the employee if forensics later clears him. The conversation on Friday at 10:00 happens only after the forensic image confirms the data on Mega.nz contains proprietary code. Until then nobody in the team knows there is an investigation.

Case Study 8

The Aquarium Thermometer

Covers: Chapter 3 (Endpoint Security), Sub-Chapters 3.1 (Unique Security Challenges of Mobile, IoT, and OT/ICS) and 3.2 (Common Vulnerabilities and the Expanded Attack Surface)

Scenario. A North American casino loses roughly 10 GB of high-roller player records. The forensic team traces the pivot: the breach started at an internet-connected fish-tank thermometer in the lobby. The thermometer is an IoT (Internet of Things) sensor placed by a vendor on the corporate Wi-Fi so it could text maintenance when the water got too cold. The IT (Information Technology) team did not know the thermometer existed; the security team's tools did not run on it. The casino's network was effectively flat — the lobby Wi-Fi could reach the staff VLAN, the staff VLAN could reach the high-roller database VLAN.

Question. Identify the three categories of failure that allowed this incident. What single control change would have stopped the lateral movement even with the thermometer compromised?

Most-possible answer. Three categories:

  • Asset inventory failure. The thermometer was not on any inventory. You cannot defend what you never inventoried — that sentence belongs on the wall of every SOC.
  • Device-class vulnerability stack. The IoT family typically ships with default credentials, unauthenticated management interfaces, unsigned firmware, and the rare-to-never firmware update — the standard catalogue from Sub-Chapter 3.2. The Mirai botnet conscripted hundreds of thousands of similar devices in 2016 by trying 61 default username/password pairs catalogued in US-CERT Alert TA16-288A.
  • Flat network design. No segmentation between the IoT VLAN, the staff VLAN, and the database VLAN.

The single control change that stops the lateral movement even with the thermometer compromised is network segmentation with strict firewall rules between zones. Put IoT devices on their own VLAN, allow only the specific outbound flows they need (DNS, a vendor cloud endpoint on TCP/443), block everything else, and forbid IoT-to-database traffic at the firewall. The thermometer can still be compromised — but the high-roller database is no longer four hops away, it is unreachable.

Case Study 9

The SCADA Workstation You Cannot Power Down

Covers: Chapter 3 (Endpoint Security), Sub-Chapter 3.3 (Adapt Incident Response Procedures for Resource-Constrained and Legacy Endpoints)

Scenario. A shop-floor engineering workstation at a Canadian manufacturing plant is showing strange outbound HTTPS traffic to an IP not on any allowed-vendor list. The workstation runs a SCADA (Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition) client that drives a process line; powering it down stops production for hours and may trip a safety system. The plant manager makes it clear that you are not pulling the plug.

Question. How does the classic NIST IR (Incident Response) lifecycle bend to fit this scenario? What collection order does the volatile-data triage follow, and which IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force) RFC (Request for Comments) defines it?

Most-possible answer. The classic NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 lifecycle adapts as follows:

  • Identification — correlate ambient network telemetry (firewall logs, NetFlow, syslog from the vendor cloud console) with the engineering workstation's own logs. The PLC behind the workstation will not tell you anything; the workstation absolutely will.
  • Containment — do not isolate the host; quarantine the path. Push an ACL (Access Control List) on the switch to drop the outbound C2 (Command and Control) channel, or block it at the upstream firewall. The device stays running, the attacker's reach is cut.
  • Eradication — there is nothing to "remove" from a PLC. On the engineering workstation you can rebuild from a known-good image during the next maintenance window; for embedded gear, the equivalent is reflash to a known-good vendor image, factory reset, and rotate any shared cryptographic material. Coordinated change, vendor on the call.
  • Recovery — the operations team decides the window, not you. Your job is to be ready when they say go.
  • Lessons learned — fix the asset inventory first. Nine times out of ten the device that got compromised was not on the list.

Volatile data is collected on the live, running workstation in order of volatility from most volatile to least volatile: CPU registers and cache, RAM (Random-Access Memory), network state (open connections, ARP table, listening ports, routing table), running processes and loaded modules, logged-on users and sessions, temporary file systems, then disk artefacts (auth log, scheduled tasks, services, recent file modifications), and finally any archival media. The reverse order destroys evidence. The order is defined in RFC 3227 — Guidelines for Evidence Collection and Archiving, published by the IETF.

Sum = 4.0 / 6.0 = 66.7 % of mapped controls satisfied. That number is exactly what you would expect on the first pass of a site that has not had a formal OT (Operational Technology) compliance program before. The number is not the deliverable; the conversation about which "no" to fix first is the deliverable. Priority recommendation: fix line 3 (cleartext data in transit) first — Modbus and many DNP3 (Distributed Network Protocol 3) deployments still run cleartext, which means any passive listener on the wire reads setpoints in real time, and that is the cheapest, most surveillable failure on the list.

MSQ 43 Chapter 3, Sub-Chapter 3.5 (Standards and Frameworks for Mobile, IoT, OT)

The IEC 62443 family is the international standard for industrial automation and control systems security. Which of the following statements about it are correct?

  1. AIt is structured as a series — 1-x (general), 2-x (policies and procedures), 3-x (system requirements), 4-x (component requirements).
  2. BIt defines seven Foundational Requirements (FR1–FR7) including Identification and Authentication Control, Use Control, System Integrity, Data Confidentiality, Restricted Data Flow, Timely Response to Events, and Resource Availability.
  3. CIt is jointly developed by IEC and the ISA — International Society of Automation.
  4. DIt is exclusively a North American standard with no European recognition.
  5. EIt defines Security Levels (SL 1–4) to express target protection capability.

A, B, C, E.

IEC is international by definition — the I stands for International. Option D is the regional-thinking trap.

MSQ 44 Chapter 3, Sub-Chapter 3.5 (Standards and Frameworks for Mobile, IoT, OT)

NIST SP 800-82 Revision 3 — Guide to Operational Technology Security — relates to NIST SP 800-53 as follows:

  1. ASP 800-53 is the general control catalogue; SP 800-82 is the OT-specific tailoring guide.
  2. BSP 800-82 helps you decide which SP 800-53 controls apply, which need adaptation for OT, and which are simply not applicable.
  3. CSP 800-82 replaces SP 800-53 for OT environments — you only need one.
  4. DSP 800-82 explicitly recognises that some IT controls — aggressive patching during business hours, for instance — would break an OT environment.
  5. ESP 800-82 is structured as a tailoring overlay, not as a competing catalogue.

A, B, D, E.

Option C is the procurement-team misunderstanding. The two documents work together: catalogue plus tailoring guide.

MSQ 45 Chapter 3, Sub-Chapter 3.5 (Standards and Frameworks for Mobile, IoT, OT)

Which of the following statements about consumer IoT baseline standards are correct?

  1. AETSI EN 303 645 is the European baseline for consumer IoT security — no default passwords, vulnerability disclosure policy, software updates, secure storage of sensitive data.
  2. BNIST IR 8259 and NIST SP 800-213 cover IoT device cybersecurity capabilities for manufacturers and federal use.
  3. C"Cite both" — when you write a procurement requirement, cite the European baseline and the United States baseline so a vendor on either side of the Atlantic understands what you mean.
  4. DIoT security baselines are forbidden by international law.
  5. ENIST SP 800-124 Revision 2 — Guidelines for Managing the Security of Mobile Devices — is the equivalent reference on the mobile side.

A, B, C, E.

Option D is absurd by design — but it is the kind of throwaway distractor you will see on certification exams. Read every option, even the obviously wrong ones.
